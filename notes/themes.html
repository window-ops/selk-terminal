Political and design context

Political and social theories behind the game's themes.

Eco-socialism and climate recovery

The concept imagines humanity bringing climate change under control through eco-socialist policy, followed by democratic socialism. Rapid emergency measures, remote institutions, and industrial interests continue to create difficult choices.

Core and periphery: who gets to build the future?

CESEA places planetary-scale research in Central and Eastern Europe. World-systems analysis and dependency theory examine how investment, technical knowledge, and decision-making concentrate in some regions, leaving others with less control over development.

The game imagines poorer regions of the Global South gaining resources and influence in science and development. Eastern Europe and the Global South have distinct histories. In this future, both can shape space research and infrastructure.

André Gunder Frank argues that underdevelopment can result from unequal economic relationships. Immanuel Wallerstein describes core, semi-periphery, and periphery as positions within a connected world economy. The game considers how broader access to advanced infrastructure could change those relationships.

Anti-imperialism, extraction, and the new frontier

The methane operation concentrates profit and decision-making on Earth while leaving ecological costs on Titan.

Lenin's analysis of imperialism focuses on monopoly, finance capital, and capital export. Dependency and anticolonial theories add other histories and perspectives. The story considers whether space expansion will reproduce extractive relationships.

Panel housing, prefabrication, and robots

The construction bots begin with a thought experiment: what if the repetitive production and assembly used in panelák, blocuri, Plattenbau, and other Eastern European prefabricated housing were automated? These terms name related regional forms with distinct histories.

The game presents prefabricated housing as an example of collective production: standard components, planning, and assembly can provide housing at scale. The robots apply this approach to construction with unfamiliar materials and in hostile environments. Their social effects depend on who sets their goals, who can change them, and whose needs they serve.

In Capital, Marx discusses how machinery changes production and how its effects depend on social control. At Selk, software changes can remove the robots' safeguards and redirect machines built for research toward extraction.

Megaprojects, systems, and hidden scale

In Selk, "mega" describes a coordinated network spread across or beneath dusty ground.

The supervisor evaluates the megaproject through delayed messages, revised entries, and misleading summaries written by organizations with competing interests.

Life beyond human ownership

The site's life-protection rules draw on planetary protection and environmental ethics. Earth's deep biosphere shows that microbial life can persist in energy-poor environments. Titan's interior remains under study, and no extraterrestrial life has been confirmed. The bacteria at Selk are fictional. The player decides what evidence is enough to protect a possible ecosystem before industrial work proceeds.
